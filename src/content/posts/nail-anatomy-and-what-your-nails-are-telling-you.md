Before I could get better at nail art, I had to understand what I was actually working with. Turns out nails are a lot more complex than "the hard part at the end of your finger" — and understanding the basics has made me both better at manicures and a little more aware of what my body might be trying to tell me.

A quick disclaimer before we dive in: I'm a hobbyist, not a medical professional. Everything below is general, educational information — the kind of thing you'd find in a wellness magazine — not a diagnosis of anything, and it's not a substitute for seeing a doctor or dermatologist. If something about your nails is worrying you, please talk to a professional who can actually look at it.

## The basic parts of a nail

A few terms that come up constantly once you start paying attention:

- **Nail plate** — the hard, visible part of the nail. Mostly made of a protein called keratin, layered together.
- **Nail bed** — the skin underneath the nail plate. This is where the nail plate is attached, and it's part of why nail color can change with circulation or health.
- **Cuticle** — the thin layer of skin at the base of the nail that seals the area where the nail grows out. It's there to protect against infection, which is why pushing it back gently (rather than aggressively cutting it) tends to be the gentler approach.
- **Lunula** — that little white, moon-shaped area at the base of some nails. Not everyone's is very visible, and that's normal too.
- **Matrix** — the part you can't see, tucked under the cuticle, where new nail cells are actually produced. This is basically the nail's engine room.

Nails grow slowly — fingernails average around 3mm a month, so a full nail typically takes several months to grow out completely. That's worth knowing because it means changes you see today may reflect something that was happening weeks or months ago.

## Common changes worth knowing about

This is the part I get asked about most, so here's a general overview — again, educational only, not diagnostic.

**Ridges.** Fine vertical ridges running from the cuticle to the tip are extremely common and usually just part of getting older, similar to skin developing fine lines. Horizontal ridges (sometimes called Beau's lines) are a bit different and can show up after an illness, injury, or period of stress, since they reflect a temporary pause in nail growth. Occasional ridges usually aren't cause for alarm, but if they're new, pronounced, or paired with other symptoms, it's worth mentioning to a doctor.

**Discoloration.** Nails can change color for lots of reasons — some as simple as nail polish staining (yes, dark reds and oranges are notorious for this, use a base coat!), and others tied to what's going on inside the body. A general rule of thumb in wellness content: if a color change is sudden, widespread, or doesn't seem tied to something obvious like polish or a bruise, it's a reasonable thing to bring up with a healthcare provider rather than guess about at home.

**Brittleness and peeling.** Nails that split, peel in layers, or break easily are often linked to things like frequent wetting and drying (hello, dishwashing), harsh acetone use, or just low humidity in winter. Keeping nails moisturized with cuticle oil and giving them breaks between polish can help a lot. If brittleness is severe or comes on suddenly, that's worth a conversation with a doctor too, since there can be other contributing factors.

**Changes in shape or texture.** Things like pitting (small dents in the nail surface) or noticeable changes in curvature are the kind of thing dermatologists are actually trained to evaluate — they're outside what any at-home routine can meaningfully address, so this is squarely "make an appointment" territory rather than "try a new cuticle oil" territory.

## The general takeaway

Nails can be a small, visible window into overall health, but they're just one data point — not a diagnostic tool, and definitely not something to self-diagnose from a blog post (including this one!). The healthiest approach I've landed on: pay attention, keep a basic care routine, and treat any sudden or persistent changes as a reason to check in with a professional rather than a Google rabbit hole.
